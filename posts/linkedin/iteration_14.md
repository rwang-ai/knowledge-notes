Software Principles in the Age of AI: Build For What, Exactly?

When I first started as an engineer, I learned the classic triangle: quality, speed, and features—you can’t fully optimize all three. I also learned more formal process ideas like PSP, including the idea that only a portion of engineering time is spent writing code, with the rest going into planning, design, review, and testing.

With AI, it’s tempting to think this model no longer applies. Feature velocity is undeniably higher. Code that once took weeks can now be produced in days—sometimes hours.

But the fundamentals haven’t disappeared. They’ve been reframed.
The classic speed–quality–features triangle hasn’t disappeared; it’s been reframed. In the age of AI, it looks more like speed of execution, quality of decisions, and clarity of business goals—with features becoming a byproduct rather than the objective.

A study discussed in The Pragmatic Engineer earlier this year found that experienced developers using an AI coding tool were, on average, ~19% slower, even though they believed they were significantly faster. The study is now about five months old—and in the age of AI, that’s a long time—but the underlying insight still holds: leverage from AI is not automatic. It depends on how and why the tool is used. https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/engineering-productivity

That leads to a more useful question than “Does AI make engineers faster?”

Faster at what?

Are you building:
- a prototype to explore an idea?
- something to communicate with stakeholders or customers?
- a system close to production?
- or software tightly coupled to real users, real data, and real business consequences?

AI is incredibly effective when you’re far from reality—early exploration, prototyping, alignment, and learning. It collapses ambiguity and shortens feedback loops.

As you move closer to reality, the work changes. The bottleneck is no longer writing code. It’s decision quality: defining system boundaries, choosing data models, understanding failure modes, and owning long-term consequences. Velocity alone stops being the advantage.

AI lowers the cost of building.
It does not lower the cost of being wrong.

The real skill in the age of AI isn’t shipping code quickly—it’s knowing what you’re building for, and when slowing down is the highest-leverage move.
