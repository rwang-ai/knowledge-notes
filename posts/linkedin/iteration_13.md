Learning in Public Series #13

Engineer, Manager, Product Thinker, Learner — Finding the Right AI-assisted Mode

I’ve been wondering whether there are documented best practices for engineers using AI—both short-term and long-term.

My own journey is tangled with a role transition: from managing a 12-person engineering team back to being an IC. Early on, I grew impatient. AI created an illusion of speed as I shifted from heavy infra work to product work.

With AI, I could ship app features in days instead of weeks. Without it, I would have taken the traditional route: learning a new stack, understanding the codebase deeply, and deciding carefully where to make changes. AI collapses that process into a much smaller scoped problem. You can leverage general programming knowledge to navigate a new stack, language, or codebase surprisingly well.

That’s a real short-term gain—and there’s nothing wrong with using it.

The tradeoff is that real learning still comes from understanding nuances: the tech stack, language idioms, and design and data model choices embedded in the codebase. Over time, I started to see this as different modes of operating:

The Modes
- Manager mode: Use AI to move fast, delegate reasoning, and get things working. Great for prototyping, alignment and unblocking.
- Engineer mode: Slow down. Decide which changes are worth writing manually. Evaluate tradeoffs by reading code, inspecting data models, or reasoning through constraints—sometimes with AI, sometimes without.
- Learner mode: Use LLMs to learn any domain in place—new languages, unfamiliar systems, or entirely new fields—while staying grounded in first principles.
- Product thinker mode: Use AI to reason about user value, product tradeoffs, and system behavior, not just implementation details.

In the pre-LLM world, the feedback loop in a new domain was long. You often had to invest weeks of effort before seeing any payoff, and it was hard to make learning feel rewarding.

Now that loop is dramatically shortened. You can experience progress almost immediately, which makes learning far more accessible—and far more enjoyable. If you love learning, LLMs can easily create an oversupply of dopamine.

My current guardrail is going offline more—back to Kindle, back to books. Less inputs, deeper digestion. Despite all the uncertainty AI introduces, the learning itself has never been more fun. The challenge now isn’t access to knowledge—it’s choosing when to slow down enough to actually absorb it.
